Why cooking conversions need ingredients

Volume-to-volume conversions are fixed: 1 cup is 16 tablespoons, 48 teaspoons, 236.6 millilitres or 8 fluid ounces, everywhere, every time. The trouble starts when recipes cross between volume and weight. A cup of flour weighs about 125 grams while a cup of honey weighs about 340 — same cup, nearly triple the weight. Any converter that turns cups into grams without asking what ingredient is guessing.

This converter uses standard densities for common ingredients: all-purpose flour 125 g/cup, granulated sugar 200 g/cup, butter 227 g/cup, water and milk about 237–245 g/cup, honey 340 g/cup, rice 185 g/cup and rolled oats 90 g/cup. Pick your ingredient and the volume-to-weight conversions use the right density.

Weighing beats scooping for accuracy. A cup of flour can vary from 100 to 150 grams depending on how packed it is — a 50 percent swing that ruins delicate baking. A cheap kitchen scale removes the variance entirely, which is why professional bakers work in grams. When a recipe gives both, always trust the weight.

Watch out for US versus metric cups: a US cup is 236.6 ml while a metric cup (used in Australia and many Commonwealth recipes) is 250 ml — a 6 percent difference that matters in baking. Tablespoons differ too: 14.8 ml in the US versus 20 ml in Australia. This converter uses US customary units; if your recipe is Australian or British, check which cup it means first.

Frequently asked questions

How many grams are in a cup of flour?

About 125 g for all-purpose flour, spooned and levelled. It varies with packing, which is why weighing is more accurate.

How many tablespoons are in a cup?

16 tablespoons (US). Also 48 teaspoons or 236.6 millilitres.

Why do cups convert to different gram weights?

Because ingredients have different densities — a cup of honey (~340 g) weighs nearly 3x a cup of flour (~125 g).

Should I measure flour by weight or volume?

By weight. Scooping packs flour unpredictably; 125 g on a scale is exact every time.
