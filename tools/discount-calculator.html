How discounts really work

A single discount is simple: multiply the price by the discount rate to get the savings, then subtract. A $120 jacket at 25 percent off saves $30, so you pay $90. The formula is final price = original price x (1 - discount / 100). This calculator applies it instantly and shows both the price you pay and the money you keep.

Stacked discounts trip people up. When a store offers an extra 10 percent off an already discounted price, the discounts multiply rather than add. A $100 item at 20 percent off, then 10 percent off the $80 sale price, costs $72 — a total saving of 28 percent, not 30 percent. Retailers know that 20% + 10% sounds bigger than 28% off, which is exactly why they phrase it that way.

Sale signs use a few other tricks worth knowing. Was/now pricing anchors you to the original price even when nobody paid it for months. Percentage discounts look bigger on expensive items while fixed-amount discounts ($20 off) look bigger on cheap ones — compare the final price, not the framing. And buy-one-get-one-half-off is just 25 percent off two items, not 50 percent off.

To judge whether a deal is genuinely good, compare the final price against the item's usual street price rather than the claimed was price. Price-tracking tools and a quick search reveal whether that 40 percent off is real or inflated. This calculator gives you the exact numbers; a thirty-second comparison tells you whether they are actually a bargain.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a discount price?

Multiply the original price by (1 - discount / 100). A $80 item at 25% off costs $80 x 0.75 = $60.

Do stacked discounts add up?

No — they multiply. 20% off followed by 10% off gives 28% total savings (0.8 x 0.9 = 0.72), not 30%.

What is the final price after two discounts?

Apply each discount to the running price in turn. Enter both percentages in the calculator above to see the result.

Why do stores show was/now prices?

The original price anchors your expectations, making the discount feel larger. Always compare the final price to the usual street price.
