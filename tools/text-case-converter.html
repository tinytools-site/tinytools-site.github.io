Text cases explained

Different contexts call for different capitalization. Uppercase shouts in headings and warnings but is hard to read in long passages. Lowercase suits code, URLs and stylized design. Title case — capitalizing the major words — is the standard for English headlines and book titles, while sentence case (capitalizing only the first word) matches normal prose and is common in user interfaces.

Title case has a subtle rule worth knowing: short conjunctions, articles and prepositions (and, the, of, in, on) usually stay lowercase unless they are the first or last word. This converter applies that rule for common short words, which handles the vast majority of headlines correctly. Legal and academic styles have their own variations, so always check a style guide for formal work.

The alternating case (“aLtErNaTiNg”) is a novelty style popular in memes and playful social posts — included here for fun rather than formal use. The inverse case flips every letter's capitalization, which is handy when you accidentally type with Caps Lock on. Extra-space cleanup collapses multiple spaces and trims lines, useful for pasted text.

Frequently asked questions

What is Title Case?

Capitalizing the first letter of each major word, e.g. “The Quick Brown Fox”. Short words like “and” or “the” usually stay lowercase.

How do I fix text typed in all caps?

Use the lowercase or sentence-case mode to normalize it in one click — much faster than retyping.

What is Sentence case?

Only the first letter of each sentence is capitalized, like normal prose: “The quick brown fox jumps.”

Is there a character limit?

No. The converter handles anything from a single word to a full document, all in your browser.
