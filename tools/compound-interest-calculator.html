The power of compound interest

Compound interest means earning interest on your interest. In year one, a $10,000 deposit at 7 percent earns $700. In year two you earn 7 percent on $10,700, which is $749 — and the snowball keeps accelerating. Over 30 years that single $10,000 deposit grows to about $81,000 without you adding another cent. The formula is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where n is how often interest compounds each year.

Time matters more than the interest rate. Starting ten years earlier beats earning a higher rate later, because compounding needs years to work its magic. This is why financial advisers repeat the same advice: start early, even with small amounts. A 25-year-old investing $200 a month at 7 percent has about $525,000 at 65; waiting until 35 cuts that to roughly $245,000 — less than half, for missing only the first decade.

Regular contributions supercharge the effect. Adding $200 a month to that $10,000 deposit at 7 percent turns the 30-year result from about $81,000 into roughly $325,000. The contributions themselves total $82,000, so compounding contributed about $243,000 of pure growth. Consistency beats timing: automatic monthly transfers remove willpower from the equation.

The Rule of 72 gives a quick mental estimate: divide 72 by your interest rate to get the years needed to double your money. At 7 percent, money doubles roughly every 10.3 years; at 4 percent, every 18 years. This calculator does the exact math with your compounding frequency, but the rule is handy for sanity-checking any projection in seconds.

Frequently asked questions

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt): principal times one plus the periodic rate, raised to the number of compounding periods. Monthly contributions add a second growing term.

What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate doubling time in years. At 6%, money doubles in about 12 years.

Does compounding frequency matter much?

Somewhat — monthly compounding beats annual compounding slightly at the same nominal rate. The rate and time horizon matter far more.

Does this account for inflation or taxes?

No. Results are nominal and pre-tax. Real purchasing power will be lower after inflation, and investment gains may be taxed.
